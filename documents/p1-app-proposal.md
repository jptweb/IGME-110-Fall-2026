## Overview

You'll work in a small group to write a business proposal for a product that doesn't exist yet.

The product can be a phone app, a web app, a desktop program, a website, or a physical device. It
can relate to games, media, campus life, or a problem you just happen to care about. Pick an SFW
topic everyone on the team can agree on. If your group is stuck coming up with something, tell me
early and we'll sort it out.

**The idea is not the hard part.** The hard part is making a case that someone should actually
fund this. That means research, evidence, and taking your own weak spots seriously before a reader
finds them.

### Some examples from previous years

| Product | What it was |
|---|---|
| *Bike Spikes* | A physical traction control solution for bicycles on winter roads |
| *BetWise* | An app to help people with gambling addiction |
| *UniAid* | A web portal aggregating student support resources |
| *Grub Guru* | A recipe finding app |
| *Swindle Spotter* | Scam detection for older users |
| *NetPet* | A robotic companion, a physical product |
| *Excuse Genie* | An app for generating excuses |
| *Taser Bed* | A bed that shocks you awake, for oversleepers |

That last one was real, and it got a good grade. Ambition and silliness are both fine. Sloppy
research is not.

## The proposal template

There are exactly two Google Docs you need, and they do different jobs. This trips people up every

| Doc | What it's for | What you do with it |
|---|---|---|
| **[Proposal Template](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1khRFXrBf5BCrPiw7z9wpl2QXw2PDqvH8JMCC1MXbbSA/copy)** | The empty document your proposal goes in | **Copy it.** One copy per team. |
| **[Template Instructions](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1AX62N88jqIE92HUXSzVwGizRtOTt2CcmOuSqwnApOEY/)** | Explains what belongs in each section of the template | **Read it.** Do not copy or edit it. |

One member of your team opens the template link, makes the copy, then shares it with the team as
**Editor** and with me as **Commenter**. Everyone works in that one document.

### What the proposal has to contain

Six sections, in this order. The [rubric](#rubric) at the bottom of this page tells you what full
credit looks like for each one.

1. **Executive Summary.** Product name, what it is, who it's for, what problem it solves.
2. **Market Research and Need Analysis.** Evidence the problem is real, and a look at what already
   exists. This is the section that separates a proposal from a pitch.
3. **Product Description.** Features, how it works, what using it feels like. Includes at least
   three mockups or wireframes.
4. **Issues and Counterarguments.** The objections a skeptical reader would raise, and your honest
   responses.
5. **Conclusion and Recommendations.** What happens next, and why it matters.
6. **Appendix.** Your citations, plus anything supporting.

## 2. Draft and Critiques

**Due Friday, September 11, 11:59 PM | Group submission | 25 points**

This deliverable has two halves that submit together: **your draft**, and **the critiques your team
writes of two other teams' drafts**.

### Before class on 3B

Have a real draft in your proposal template. Not finished, but far enough along that another team
can react to it. A draft with three empty sections wastes your reviewers' time and gets you nothing
useful back.

**Bring it to class.** You don't submit the draft separately.

### In class on 3B: the critiques

Your team critiques **two** other teams' drafts. Assignments are posted with the
[team list](p1-teams.md).

**→ [Peer critique questions](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1CAPF8K9el4CezmS6bWnHoLaBGgV3lhGh40rxWIcxGd8/)**

For each team you review:

1. Create a Google Doc named `Feedback for team [#], from team [#], section [#]`
2. Share it with all of RIT
3. Copy the questions from the link above into it
4. Pick **one scribe** for your team

Then, for each question: **read** the relevant part of their proposal, **discuss** it as a group,
**agree** on an answer, and the scribe types your consensus.

> **Do not split the questions up between people.** The value here is the argument your team has on
> the way to an answer. Divide it up and you get six disconnected opinions instead of one useful
> review. I can tell the difference immediately.

Post each finished critique link in the Slack thread **`Week 3B: P1 Feedback Links`** as you go, so
the team you reviewed can start using it right away.

### What to submit

**One submission per team**, containing three links:

- Your team's draft proposal
- Your critique of the first team
- Your critique of the second team

### How this is graded

Mostly on the critiques. **A generous, specific, slightly uncomfortable critique is worth more than
a polite one.** "Looks good, maybe add more detail" is not feedback. Telling a team their market
research doesn't actually support their central claim is feedback, and it's the thing that makes
their final version better.

## Rubric

What a 100% looks like on the final proposal.

### 1. Executive Summary
- Clear, concise product name and description
- Target audience is well defined and specific
- Key problem and unique features are clearly articulated
- Professional writing quality

### 2. Market Research and Need Analysis
- Strong evidence of research with multiple credible sources
- Clear competitor analysis with specific examples
- Statistical data or qualitative evidence included
- Visual elements present, such as charts or competitor screenshots
- Sources properly referenced

### 3. Product Description
- Comprehensive feature list with clear explanations
- UI and UX considerations well described
- Creative or innovative elements highlighted
- At least three visual mockups or wireframes included

### 4. Issues and Counterarguments
- Thoughtful consideration of multiple potential objections
- Technical and financial challenges identified
- Reasonable responses to challenges provided
- Critical thinking evident

### 5. Conclusion and Recommendations
- Strong summary of key points
- Clear articulation of potential impact
- Compelling call to action
- Reinforces main arguments effectively

### 6. Appendix
- Properly formatted citations present
- Additional supporting materials included

## Writing advice

Things that reliably cost people points:

- **Use formatting.** Headings, subheadings, and lists make an argument easy to follow. Long
  unbroken blocks of text are hard to read and readers skim them.
- **Get the tone right.** You're persuading a professional audience. Not jokey, not flippant.
  Minimal slang, no expletives.
- **Skip interjections.** ([What's an interjection?](https://www.thoughtco.com/what-is-an-interjection-1691178))
- **Less is usually more.** Cut redundancy. If two sentences say the same thing, keep the better
  one.

On AI: the course policy applies here. Using it is fine, hiding it is not. It's a genuinely useful
research and drafting partner for a document like this. It's also confidently wrong about facts,
and a proposal whose market research cites a study that doesn't exist is worse than one with no
market research at all. Check what it tells you.
