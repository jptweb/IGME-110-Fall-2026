## Overview

You'll make a **60 to 90 second video advertisement**, working on your own, selling one of the
products the class prototyped in Project 3.

This is the last project of the semester, and it's deliberately the one where you stop researching
and start selling. Everything you need already exists: the prototype, the screens, the audience it
was designed for, and what real users said about it when they tested it. Your job is to turn that
into a minute of video that makes someone want the thing.

Three deliverables, and they build on each other. You plan the video, you cut a rough version, then
you finish it. You will not be graded on being a good filmmaker. You'll be graded on whether the ad
is aimed at a real audience, whether it holds together, and whether you actually did the editing.

> **This is an individual project.** Project 3 was your last group work. Nobody else's schedule is
> going to wreck your Thanksgiving.

## Picking a prototype

After the Project 3 presentations, every team's deck and prototype goes on the
[P4 Prototypes page](p4-prototypes.md). Pick one from that list.

**It does not have to be your own team's**, and it does not have to be from your section. Most of
the class picks somebody else's, which is the interesting version of this assignment: you're a
person hired to sell a product you didn't design, which is what advertising actually is.

You are selling the product the prototype represents. You are not reviewing the prototype, and you
are not making a demo video that walks through its screens.

## What the video has to contain

Every version you hand in, rough or final, is built from the same parts:

- **An opening that hooks.** You have about three seconds before someone decides whether to keep
  watching.
- **The key feature or experience.** At least one concrete thing the product does, shown rather
  than described.
- **A close.** A call to action, or a closing image somebody remembers.
- **A title card** naming the product.
- **Credits** for every asset you didn't make yourself. Images, footage, music, sound effects, and
  anything generated by AI.

The final version adds **captions**, which are required. See [deliverable 3](#3-final-video).

**Length is 60 to 90 seconds on the final.** The rough cut doesn't have to hit that exactly, but it
does have to be long enough to show the pacing you're going for.

## The software

Two class sessions in week 13 exist to get you ready for this, and both are graded activities:

| Day | Session | What you get out of it |
|---|---|---|
| 13A, Tue Nov 17 | [Audio Editing with Audacity](../activities/audio-editing-with-audacity.md) | Recording, levels, noise reduction, exporting a track you can drop into a video |
| 13B, Thu Nov 19 | [Video Production](../activities/video-production.md) | Cutting picture to an existing soundtrack, which is exactly the shape of this project |

**Shotcut is the recommended editor.** It's free, it runs on Windows, Mac, and Linux, and it does
everything this project needs. iMovie, CapCut, and DaVinci Resolve are all fine if you already know
one of them. Do not spend three days learning a new editor for a 90 second video.

Audio is the part people underestimate. A shaky shot gets forgiven. Clipping, hiss, and a voiceover
quieter than the music behind it do not.

## AI Expectations

This project exists partly to put you in an editing timeline, because that skill carries into 235,
340, and any job that touches media. Handing that part to a tool skips the thing being taught.

**You may use AI for:**

- Brainstorming your concept, script, or angle
- Generating or finding background music
- Creating graphics, images, or short clips to use as assets
- Proofreading your work plan or reflection

**You must do these yourself, in editing software:**

- Assembling footage and images on a timeline
- Every cut, transition, and timing decision
- Mixing and adjusting audio levels
- Exporting the finished video

**You may not use:**

- AI video generation to produce your whole video or a complete sequence
- Auto-edit features that assemble the video for you

**Every deliverable requires a screenshot of your editing timeline.** That's how this is checked,
and it's why the screenshot is worth points rather than being a formality.

**Credit AI-generated assets in your credits**, the same as any other asset you didn't make. And
per the syllabus, when you use AI on something you hand in, name the tool and say in a sentence
what you used it for. No transcript needed.

> Not disclosing AI use is an academic integrity violation under
> [RIT policy D08.0](https://www.rit.edu/policies/d080). Using these tools inside the rules above is
> not a problem. Hiding it is.

## 1. Work Plan

**Due Friday, November 20, 11:59 PM | Individual submission | 100 points**

Before you shoot or cut anything, decide what you're making and who it's for.

### What to do

Write a document with five parts. They're the five things the rubric grades, in order.

1. **Prototype Selection.** Which prototype you picked, and why you picked it. What about it did
   you think you could sell?
2. **Target Audience.** Who this ad is aimed at. Be specific. "People who play this style of game"
   is too broad to design anything against. Age, situation, what they already use, what they're
   frustrated by. The Project 3 team already did audience work and user testing, so start from
   what they found rather than inventing it.
3. **Video Type and Style.** Which kind of ad you're making and what tone it takes: funny,
   dramatic, straightforward, aspirational. Say why that choice fits the audience you just
   described. [This breakdown of video marketing types](https://quickframe.com/blog/best-video-marketing/)
   is a good place to start if you don't know what the options are.
4. **Advertisement Outline.** A storyboard or a script. Scene by scene, with rough timings, showing
   what's on screen and what's on the audio track. **This is the biggest single piece of the
   grade**, and it's the part that tells me whether your idea fits in 90 seconds.
5. **Project Schedule.** What you're doing in which week between now and December 11. Thanksgiving
   is in the middle of this project. Plan around it rather than being surprised by it.

Add an **AI disclosure** at the end if you used AI on any of the above.

> **See the [sample work plan](p4-work-plan-sample.md)** for the level of detail expected. It's a
> structural guide, not a template to fill in. The thinking has to be yours.

### What to submit

Hand in **one file** on myCourses: a `.docx` or `.pdf` containing all five parts.

> **Why this exists.** Almost every video that goes badly went badly here, when somebody picked a
> prototype they weren't interested in and wrote three sentences about "young adults aged 18 to 34."
> The storyboard is also a scope check. If your outline needs four locations and a cast, we find
> that out now rather than the week it's due.

## 2. Rough Cut

**Due Friday, December 4, 11:59 PM | Individual submission | 100 points**

A version of the ad that's clearly the ad, with the boring parts unfinished.

### What to do

1. **Cut a rough version** from your work plan. It needs an opening, at least one key feature, and
   enough material to show the pacing. It does not need to be 60 to 90 seconds yet.
2. **Use placeholders freely.** Storyboard drawings standing in for footage you haven't shot. A
   text-to-speech scratch track instead of the real voiceover. Static frames where an animation
   goes. All fine, all expected.
3. **Put in a title card and placeholder credits.** Even if the credits are three lines that say
   "music TBD."
4. **Write a reflection**, 150 to 200 words: what's done, what still needs real work, and what
   you're changing from your original plan.
5. **Include your updated storyboard**, showing anything that changed since the work plan.

### What to submit

Three things on myCourses:

- The **rough cut video file**
- The **reflection document**, `.docx` or `.pdf`, with the updated storyboard in it
- A **screenshot of your editing timeline**

### How this is graded

Progress and evidence, not polish. The rubric weights **effort and progress** highest, then the
core elements being present, then the reflection, the timeline screenshot, and the title card and
credits. A rough cut that's honestly rough and clearly moving scores well. A polished 20 seconds
does not.

> **Why this exists.** This is the checkpoint that catches the two failure modes: nothing started,
> and something started that can't possibly be finished. Both are fixable on December 4 and neither
> is fixable on December 10.

## 3. Final Video

**Due Friday, December 11, 11:59 PM | Individual submission | 100 points**

### What to do

1. **Watch your rough cut again**, along with any feedback you got on it, and list what needs to
   change.
2. **Finish it to 60 to 90 seconds**, with a real opening, the key feature, and a real close.
3. **Replace every placeholder.** Final visuals, final audio, transitions and pacing that feel
   deliberate.
4. **Add captions.** Required, and worth as much as any other part of the video. Three ways to do
   it, pick one:

   | Option | How | Note |
   |---|---|---|
   | **Open captions** | Text overlays burned into the video in your editor | Most control over timing and look |
   | **Caption file** | Export a separate `.srt` and hand it in alongside the `.mp4` | Viewers can toggle it off |
   | **YouTube** | Upload unlisted to your `@g.rit.edu` account, let YouTube auto-caption, then correct every error by hand | Read the warning below |

   If you take the YouTube route, the captions still have to be **correct**. Auto-captions are good
   and not perfect, and obvious uncorrected errors cost points. Fix them in YouTube Studio under
   Subtitles. **Hand in the `.mp4` either way.** Use your RIT account rather than a personal one.

   [Open vs. closed captions, and how to edit them](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DTf_ucW7h8w)

5. **Finish the title card and credits.** Credits cover every asset you used, including anything
   AI-generated.
6. **Write the final reflection**, 250 to 300 words: what you changed from the rough cut and why,
   what was hard, and what you learned about making an ad. Attach your **finalized storyboard** as
   an appendix, even if it barely changed.

### What to submit

On myCourses:

- The **`.mp4`**, 60 to 90 seconds. Required no matter which caption route you took.
- Your **`.srt` file**, if you went that way. If you used YouTube, paste the unlisted link in the
  comments field on your submission instead.
- The **reflection document**, `.docx` or `.pdf`, with the finalized storyboard as an appendix
- A **screenshot of your editing timeline**

### How this is graded

Five things about the video itself carry most of it: content and communication, visual and audio
quality, structure and pacing, captions, and the title card and credits. The rest is checkable
rather than arguable: the `.mp4` is there or it isn't, the timeline screenshot is there or it
isn't, the reflection covers its three parts, the finalized storyboard is attached.

## Where this goes next

Video is the one thing in this course that shows up everywhere afterward and never gets its own
class. You'll cut a demo reel for a portfolio, a walkthrough for a 340 app, a pitch video for a
capstone, or a 30 second thing for a client who asked for it on Friday.

The habit worth keeping isn't Shotcut. It's that you storyboarded before you shot, and that when
the rough cut showed you the idea didn't fit in 90 seconds, you changed the idea instead of the
deadline.
