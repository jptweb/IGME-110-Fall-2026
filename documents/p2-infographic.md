## Overview

You'll make one infographic, working on your own, explaining something about a product the class
proposed in Project 1.

An infographic exists to make data understandable at a glance. So the work here is not decoration.
You'll take research that already exists in a Project 1 proposal, almost certainly add research of
your own, decide what story that data tells, and then design something that tells it to a specific
person.

Three deliverables. You plan it, you draft it and get it critiqued, then you finish it.

> **This is individual work.** Your Project 1 group does not carry over.

## Picking a proposal

Every team's product and proposal document is listed on the [Project 1 teams page](p1-teams.md).
Pick one.

**It does not have to be your own team's**, and most people pick somebody else's. Read a few before
you decide. You're looking for one with enough substance behind it that you have something to
visualize.

**You are not summarizing the whole proposal.** Pick an angle. Using the product's name as your
title is not enough on its own, because it doesn't tell anyone what they're about to look at. Some
angles that work:

- What the product does, feature by feature
- How it compares to what already exists
- How that kind of product has changed over time
- What the process of using it looks like
- Who the market is, and what they currently do instead

## What the infographic must contain

Same requirements for the mockup and the final. The difference is how finished they are.

**1. A title** that is legible and makes the subject clear at a glance. See
[Picking a proposal](#picking-a-proposal) on why the product name alone doesn't do this.

**2. At least five elements.** Each one labeled clearly enough that a reader scanning the
infographic gets the point without reading closely.

**3. An image for every element.** The images have to look like they belong together, and they have
to be recognizable at a glance. Photographs are usually the wrong choice here. You want clean, simple
graphics. [The Noun Project](https://thenounproject.com/) and [Font Awesome](https://fontawesome.com)
are good sources, and Canva and Adobe Express have libraries built in.

**4. Color, type, and imagery that hold together.** This is where your mood board pays off. If you've
changed direction since the Plan, that's fine, as long as the new direction is also a direction.

**Two things that cost people marks every year:**

- **Leaving the template as it came.** If you start from a Canva template and the placeholder icons,
  colors, or dummy text are still in there, that reads as unfinished, and the rubric treats it that
  way.
- **Screenshotting charts out of the proposal.** They come in at the wrong colors and go blurry when
  scaled. Rebuild them in Canva or PowerPoint so they match your palette.

**Format:** submit the infographic as a **PDF**. Not a PNG, not a link. The rubric checks this
first.

There's no required size or shape. If you have a display context in mind, say so in your
documentation. Type that works on a bus shelter poster will not work on a page someone reads on a
laptop, and telling me what you were designing for helps me grade what you actually made.

## Citing your sources

Your documentation carries a sources section, and it grows across the mockup and the final. Cite:

1. **The tool** you built the infographic in. Canva, Photoshop, Illustrator, whichever.
2. **Your typefaces**, with links. If the font is built into Canva or Photoshop, look it up on
   [Google Fonts](https://fonts.google.com) or [Adobe Fonts](https://fonts.adobe.com) and cite that.
3. **Every image**, the same way you cited sources in Project 1.
4. **Every data source, including the proposal you chose.** A link to the proposal document is
   fine. Anything you researched yourself gets a real citation.
5. **Any images you generated with AI**, following the
   [MLA guidance on citing generative AI](https://style.mla.org/citing-generative-ai-updated-revised/).

[ZoteroBib](https://zbib.org/) and the [RIT Library Citation Guide](https://infoguides.rit.edu/citation)
are the tools for this, same as Project 1.

> **Number 4 is the one people skip.** This is a project about presenting data. Where the data came
> from is part of the work, not an afterthought.

## AI Expectations

**You may use AI for:**

- Brainstorming angles, or working out what story your data tells
- Generating images and icons, cited as above
- Proofreading your documentation

**You must do yourself:**

- The design. Layout, type, color, and the decisions behind them.
- Choosing and verifying your data. If a tool hands you a statistic, find where it came from before
  you put it in front of a reader.

**You may not** generate the finished infographic and hand it in as your design work.

Per the syllabus, when you use AI on something you hand in, name the tool and say in a sentence
what you used it for. No transcript needed.

> Not disclosing AI use is an academic integrity violation under
> [RIT policy D08.0](https://www.rit.edu/policies/d080).

## 1. Plan

**Due Friday, October 2, 11:59 PM | Individual submission | 25 points**

A written plan. No infographic yet.

### What to do

Write a document with four sections.

1. **Proposal Selection.** Which proposal you picked and why. Not "it looked interesting." What
   about it gave you something to visualize, and what angle are you taking on it?
2. **Audience and Goals.** Who this is for, and what you want it to do: inform, persuade, compare,
   explain. Be specific about the person. The
   [Five Ws of Success](https://www.linkedin.com/learning/learning-infographic-design-14154168/defining-the-5-ws)
   video covers what to nail down, and the "What" and "Who" sections are the ones that matter here.
3. **Data Requirements.** What data you need to make your point. How much of it is already in the
   proposal, and what you'll have to go find. Data takes more forms than you might expect:
   statistics, timelines, feature comparisons, process flows, market research, user demographics.
4. **Mood Board.** Your design direction, built out from the one you made in class on 6A. Include
   typography, a color palette, images and icons in the style you're going for, and a couple of
   reference infographics that influenced you.

### What to submit

**One file** on myCourses, `.docx` or `.pdf`, with all four sections.

Export your mood board as a **PNG or JPEG** and embed it in the document. Don't link out to it.

> **Why this exists.** The people who struggle with the mockup are almost always the ones who
> skipped straight to opening Canva. Deciding who it's for and what data you have is twenty minutes
> that saves you a redesign.

## 2. Mockup

**Due Friday, October 9, 11:59 PM | Individual submission | 50 points**

A complete draft of the infographic, rough but whole.

### What to do

1. **Build a draft** that meets everything in
   [what the infographic must contain](#what-the-infographic-must-contain). Title, five labeled
   elements, an image each, a coherent look.
2. **Bring it to class on 7B**, Thursday October 8. You'll show it to two or three classmates and
   get feedback on it in the
   [Infographic Peer Feedback activity](../activities/infographic-peer-feedback.md), which is
   graded separately.
3. **Write your documentation**, answering these five questions:
   - Who is the audience for this?
   - What do you want it to accomplish?
   - What type of infographic did you use, and why?
   - How do the design elements (color, images, typeface) focus the design on your audience and goal?
   - Why did you lay the elements out the way you did?
4. **Add your sources section**, per [citing your sources](#citing-your-sources).

### What to submit

Two files on myCourses:

- The **infographic as a PDF**
- Your **documentation**, `.docx` or `.pdf`, with the five answers and the sources

### How this is graded

**On completeness, not on quality.** Every required piece present and documented scores well even if
the design still needs work. That's the point of a draft.

Take it seriously anyway. This is your only round of feedback before the final, and the peer session
on 7B happens the day before this is due, so what you hear can still make it into what you hand in.

> **Why this exists.** Designs are never right the first time. Finding out on October 8 that nobody
> can tell what your infographic is about is a good outcome. Finding out on October 16 is not.

## 3. Final Infographic

**Due Friday, October 16, 11:59 PM | Individual submission | 100 points**

> ⚠️ **October Break is Monday and Tuesday of this week.** You get one class meeting, on Thursday
> October 15, between the mockup and this. Plan the long weekend accordingly rather than counting
> on the week.

### What to do

1. **Act on the feedback** you got on 7B. You don't have to take all of it, but you do have to have
   thought about it.
2. **Finish the design.** The title is clear and relevant. The type is legible and suits the
   audience. The colors work together. The images are consistent across the whole piece.
3. **Check every element.** For all five: the content is relevant, the content and label are
   legible, the image fits the content, and the image is recognizable at a glance.
4. **Rebuild any charts** you carried over from the proposal so they match your palette and are
   sharp at full size.
5. **Update your documentation.** Everything from the mockup, plus:
   - How the feedback you received changed your design
   - All sources, updated for anything you added

Writing quality counts on the final. Grammar, spelling, and structure are part of the grade.

### What to submit

Two files on myCourses:

- The **final infographic as a PDF**
- Your **documentation**, `.docx` or `.pdf`

### How this is graded

Three groups. **The design as a whole**: PDF format, title, typography, color, imagery. **The five
elements**: content relevance, legibility, and whether each image fits and reads at a glance.
**The documentation**: writing quality, how clearly you defined audience and goals, how well you
explained your format and design choices, and what you did with the feedback.

## Rubric

The full rubrics are attached to each assignment in myCourses. This is what they weight.

**1. Plan (25).** Five things, weighted evenly: proposal choice, the goal, the audience, data
requirements, and the mood board.

**2. Mockup (50).** Presence and completeness. Is it an infographic, does the title relate to the
product, are all five elements there with a description and an image, is the documentation complete,
are the sources cited. Elements that still carry template placeholder content score as partial.

**3. Final Infographic (100).** Overall design, then the five elements, then the document.

## Where this goes next

This is the only project in the course where the whole deliverable is one image, and it's the one
that most directly rehearses something you'll do constantly later: taking something you understand
and making it legible to somebody who doesn't.

The habit worth keeping is the order you did it in. Audience, then data, then design. Almost every
infographic that fails, fails because it was designed first and aimed at nobody.
