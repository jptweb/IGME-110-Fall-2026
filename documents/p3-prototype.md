## Overview

In a team of about three, you'll design an app for one of the products the class proposed in
Project 1. You'll sketch a rough version, put it in front of people who have never seen it, and then
build a clickable version that fixes what they ran into.

That's the UX process in four weeks: decide who it's for, sketch it, test it, fix it. If you take
IGME-236 you'll run the same loop again, slower and deeper. This is the first lap, so the goal is to
get all the way around once and see how the pieces connect, not to do any one of them perfectly.

**The test is the part that matters most.** Watching somebody who isn't you try to use your design
is uncomfortable, and it's where most of the learning in this project happens. A team whose lo-fi
falls apart on test day, and who fixes it properly afterward, has done this project well.

> **You're building a prototype, not an app.** Nothing has to work behind the screens. If a button
> is supposed to save something, it just goes to a screen that says "Saved."

## Picking a product

Your team picks one product from the [Project 1 teams page](p1-teams.md). Any team's, from either
section. It can be one somebody on your team helped propose, and more than one team can pick the
same product.

**Pick something you can picture a person using on a screen.** A product with a clear person and a
clear moment ("the washer I wanted is taken and I have class in an hour") is much easier to
prototype than a big platform that does everything.

If the product is a physical device, prototype its app: the screen somebody would use to set it up,
control it, or check on it.

Stuck? The [Project Roulette Wheel](https://people.rit.edu/~jxtadm/110picker/) still works. Read
whatever it lands on before you commit to it.

The proposal you pick already has research in it about who the product is for. Use it. Your
personas should come out of that research.

## AI Expectations

This project is about design decisions and watching real people use them. Those have to be yours.

**You may use AI for:**

- Brainstorming features, or ways a persona might use the product
- Generating persona photos (credit them like any other image)
- Filler content inside a screen, like sample recipes or usernames
- Proofreading what you write

**You must do yourselves:**

- The personas: who they are, what they want, and what gets in their way. A persona is a summary of
  research, so it has to come from the research.
- The screens and the layout
- The test tasks, the testing, and what you conclude from it

**You may not** use a tool that generates screens or a whole prototype for you, including the AI
features built into Figma, and hand that in as your design.

**Every deliverable ends with one AI line, even when the answer is no.** "We used no AI" is a
complete answer. If you did use it, name the tool and say in a sentence what you used it for. No
transcript needed.

## 1. Work Plan and Personas

**Due Sunday, October 25, 11:59 PM | One submission per team**

Pick your product, decide who it's for, and decide how your team is going to work. Thursday's
planning studio is for this.

### What to do

Make one document with five parts:

1. **The product.** Which Project 1 proposal you picked, with a link, and why. Then the core problem
   in a sentence or two: what is somebody trying to do that's hard right now?
2. **Features.** Three to five things the app does. Mark the ones your personas would use most,
   because those are what your prototype will show. You only build enough to run one test task per
   persona, which usually comes to four to six screens.
3. **Audience.** Who the product is for, in a short paragraph, drawn from the proposal's research.
4. **Three personas,** one per teammate, with the owner's name on each. Start with your main user,
   then add two others who would use the product differently. A power user and a casual user is the
   classic pair, and a good default.
5. **How you'll work.** Your roles, who owns which persona, when you'll meet each week, where you
   talk, and where your files live.

End with your AI line.

### What a persona needs

A persona is a made-up person, described like a real one. Each one is a single visual page with:

- A photo and a name
- Age and situation: student, job, whatever matters for this product
- **Goals:** two or three things they want
- **Frustrations:** two or three things in their way
- **A quote:** one line in their own voice about the problem
- **When they'd use it:** a short scenario

> *"Sarah, 20, college student. Needs to organize her schedule."* is not a persona. It's a name
> and a sentence.

Canva has persona templates, and Figma, Slides, or PowerPoint all work too. For examples, see the
[NN/g personas study guide](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/personas-study-guide/).

### What to submit

**One PDF** on myCourses, once per team, with all five parts. The personas go in it.

### How this is graded

The personas carry the most weight: are they visual, complete, believable, and grounded in the
proposal's research? Then the product and its problem, how you'll work, the audience, the features,
and whether the document is clean and easy to read.

## 2. Lo-Fi Prototype and Test Script

**Due Wednesday, November 4, 11:59 PM | One submission per team**

A rough version of the app, just real enough to test, and the script you'll test it with.

**Have every task written and every screen drawn by Tuesday's class (November 3).** The rehearsal
runs on them. Wednesday is for fixing what the rehearsal turns up and putting the PDF together.

### What to do

1. **Write your test task.** Each person picks one thing their persona would want to do in the app
   and turns it into a task for a tester, using the [test script](p3-test-script.md). A good task
   gives a reason and a goal, and doesn't say where to tap:

   | | |
   |---|---|
   | ❌ Too vague | "Use the app." |
   | ❌ Gives it away | "Tap Laundry, pick Gleason, then tap Hold." |
   | ✅ | "Your laundry is piling up and you have class in an hour. Find a washer you can use right now and hold it." |

   Under each task, write the **expected path** in one line: the screens you think a tester will go
   through, like *Home → Laundry → Gleason → Washer 4 → Held*. That's for your team. Don't read it
   out loud.

2. **Draw the screens.** Together, make every screen on your expected paths. For three tasks that's
   usually four to six. **Lo-fi means boxes, labels, and layout**: no color, no real fonts, no
   photos. Give each screen a name, and use the same names in your expected paths.

   Paper works very well here, because on test day somebody has to swap the screens by hand. If one
   screen takes you longer than 20 minutes, you're making it too nice.

Fill in the rest of the script, which is mostly already written. Put your name on your task, and end
with your AI line.

> **See the [sample lo-fi submission](p3-lofi-sample.md)** for how the tasks and screens fit
> together. It shows the shape. The content has to be yours.

### What to submit

**One PDF** on myCourses, once per team: the screens (clear photos if you drew them on paper), then
the test script with your tasks in it.

**Bring the screens to class on test day.** Paper is best. If yours are digital, print them, or be
ready to show exactly one screen at a time.

### How this is graded

Is there a task for each persona, with a reason, a goal, and no hints? Do the screens cover every
step of every expected path, and can a stranger read them? Is the script ready to run?

## 3. Testing Results

**Due Friday, November 6, 11:59 PM | One submission per team**

### Before test day

**Tuesday of week 11 is rehearsal.** Run your whole script once inside your team, with someone
swapping the paper screens, and fix whatever breaks: a missing screen, a dead end, or a task that
gives the answer away. If the rehearsal finds nothing, it probably wasn't a real one.

### On test day

On Thursday of week 11 your team pairs with another team. You test their prototype, they test
yours, and then you talk about what you saw. There are four roles:

| Role | Job |
|---|---|
| **Facilitator** | Reads the tasks and the questions. Doesn't help. "What would you do here?" is the only hint allowed. |
| **Computer** | Swaps in the next screen when the tester taps something, and does nothing else |
| **Scribe** | Writes down what the tester does and says, especially where they hesitate |
| **Tester** | Someone from the other team. Thinks out loud the whole time. |

Use one copy of the script per tester. **Aim for at least two testers.** If you can't get two in
class, find another before the deadline. A roommate or a friend who hasn't seen your app counts.

**When a tester gets stuck, the test is working.** Write down where, and let them keep going.

### What to do afterward

Write a one-page summary:

1. **Each task.** Did testers finish it? Where did they hesitate, get lost, or say something you
   didn't expect?
2. **Patterns.** Anything that happened with more than one tester.
3. **Your three fixes.** The three changes you'll make in the hi-fi, each tied to something a tester
   actually did. "Two of our three testers looked for Save at the top of the screen, so we're moving
   it there" is a fix. "Make it more intuitive" isn't.

End with your AI line.

### What to submit

**One PDF** on myCourses, once per team: the summary, then every completed script.

### How this is graded

Completed scripts from at least two testers, a summary that reports what actually happened, and
three fixes that each point to something a tester did.

## 4. Hi-Fi Prototype and Presentation

**Due Thursday, November 12, when your class starts | One submission per team**

### The prototype

Build it in Figma, clickable, starting from the home screen.

- **Every task works from start to finish.** Someone who has never seen it can do all of your test
  tasks without help.
- **Your three fixes from testing are in it.**
- **It looks like one app.** One type pairing, one color palette, the same buttons everywhere.
  Weeks 6 and 7 come back here.
- **Real content.** No lorem ipsum, and no buttons that just say "Button."

That's the whole list. You don't need to build the rest of the app, and screens that none of your
tasks use are optional. Three tasks that work beat ten screens that don't connect.

Tuesday of week 12 is a build day. In the last 15 minutes you'll swap with another team for a quick
look at each other's progress.

### The presentation

**Four minutes, then one question.** There are about ten teams in each section, so the four minutes
is firm. Everyone on the team speaks.

A shape that fits:

| Part | Time |
|---|---|
| The product and who it's for, with one persona | 30 seconds |
| What testing changed: a lo-fi screen next to its hi-fi version, and why | 1 minute |
| Live demo: do your tasks in the prototype | 2 minutes |
| Close | 30 seconds |

Keep it to four to six slides. The demo is the presentation.

**Then add appendix slides** after your last slide: your three personas and your Testing Results
summary. You won't present them. In Project 4, everyone in the class picks one of these products to
make a video ad for, and the appendix is how they'll learn who it's for and what testers said.

Present from your own laptop, or I can pull it up from your submission. Either way, have a backup.
Practice once with a timer, because four minutes goes faster than you think.

### What to submit

On myCourses, once per team, **before your class starts on Thursday**:

- The **Figma prototype link**, set so anyone with the link can view. Check it in a private window.
- The **slides**, as a PDF or PPTX, with the appendix. Put your AI line on the last slide.

### How this is graded

Mostly the prototype: does it show the product's core idea, can someone use it without help, does
it look like one app, and do the fixes from testing show? Then the presentation: clear, on time, a
demo that works, everyone speaking, and a real answer to the question.

## Where this goes next

Project 4 starts the week after you present. Everyone picks one of these prototypes to make a video
ad for, and it may well be yours. That's why the link has to open and the appendix has to be there.

Further out, every design class you take runs some version of this loop with more time at each step:
real interviews before the personas, more than one round of testing. The habit worth keeping from
this one is simple. Watch somebody use the thing before you decide it's done.
