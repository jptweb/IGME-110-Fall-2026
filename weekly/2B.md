## II. Would You Cite This?

Same game as Tuesday, now on real sources. I'll put a few in front of you, you vote on whether you'd cite it
in your proposal, and then you tell me why.

The tool for this is the **CRAAP test**. It's boring and it takes ninety seconds:

| | Ask |
|---|---|
| **C**urrency | When was it published or updated? |
| **R**elevance | Does it actually relate to your topic? |
| **A**uthority | Who wrote it, and who published it? |
| **A**ccuracy | Can you verify what it claims? |
| **P**urpose | Why does this exist? Who benefits if you believe it? |

The first one I show you will fail obviously. The second will look completely respectable and
still fail one of the five. The second kind is what you'll actually run into.

**And here's the one that catches people:** a source can be entirely real, published somewhere
legitimate, and still not say what you're citing it for. Fabricated sources are rare. Misused
real ones are constant, and they're the ones that cost you credit.

## III. Finding Something Real

I'll take a real claim from one of your teams and search for it live, badly at first. Three
habits, demonstrated rather than described:

1. **Start at the library, not a search engine.** The
   [RIT Libraries IGM InfoGuide](https://infoguides.rit.edu/game-design) is the front door.
   Study Guide 2 had you look at it. Today we use it.
2. **Change your search terms when you get nothing.** Finding nothing almost never means nothing
   exists. It means you asked wrong. This is the actual skill, and it's the step people skip.
3. **Open the full text.** If all you can see is an abstract, you can't check whether it says
   what you think it says.

Worth setting up once while you're here:
[Google Scholar signed in through RIT](https://infoguides.rit.edu/googlescholar), so it knows
what the library already gives you access to.

## IV. The Activity (Graded, Done in Class)

**→ [Full instructions: Sources and Citations](../activities/sources-and-citations.md)**

**This is Section 2 of your proposal**, built in class. Market Research and Need Analysis is the
section that has to prove the problem is real, and your draft is due Wednesday.

It runs in four steps:

1. **Your team decides what Section 2 has to prove.** Usually four claims: the problem is real,
   actual people have it, something already exists, it falls short. Those become four subheadings
   in your proposal doc, and that's your outline.
2. **Each person takes one claim** and goes and proves it. Three sources, deliberately one of each
   type: a major US newspaper article, an ACM Digital Library paper, and a **Reddit post**.
3. **Run all three through [ZoteroBib](https://zbib.org)** into APA, and **check what it hands
   back.** It gets authors and dates wrong on web pages constantly.
4. **Everyone loads their sources into the proposal doc**, under the claim they belong to.

Yes, Reddit. Not as proof, but as evidence that real people have this problem and how they talk
about it. Knowing what each kind of source is *good for* is the entire point of mixing them.

**This is individual work on a group topic.** You each hand in your own claim and your own three
sources. They won't be the same as your teammates'.

It closes tonight, and the period is long enough to finish it. That's what the time is for.

> your team didn't, that's the first two minutes of class.
