## II. The Takedown

Somebody made a video. Somebody else claimed it. We're going to look at a recent case and argue
about whether it was fair.

Three questions:

- **Was this fair?**
- **Who actually got paid?**
- **What should the creator have done instead?**

**The point:** "I found it on Google" is not a license. And the thing that enforces copyright on
most platforms is not a judge, it's an automated matching system that flags first and asks
questions later. Both halves matter to you. The law says one thing, and the robot that acts
before anyone reads the law is what will actually happen.

Project 4 is a video ad. It needs music. What you learn today is what stops that video getting
muted in December.

## III. What You're Actually Allowed to Use

You read seven things for the study guide, so I'm not going to repeat them. These are the four
distinctions people get wrong.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Copyright vs trademark vs patent** | Copyright covers the thing you made. Trademark covers what you call it. Patent covers how it works. A single app can involve all three. |
| **Public domain is not "visible on the internet"** | This is the most common mistake by a wide margin. Being able to see something and right-click it has nothing to do with whether you may use it. |
| **Fair use is a defense, not a permission** | It is what you argue in court after you have been sued. It is not a box you tick before publishing. Nobody can tell you in advance that something is definitely fair use. |
| **Creative Commons is a family of licenses, not one license** | BY, BY-SA, BY-NC, BY-ND, CC0. Two of those letters matter most to you: **NC** means non-commercial, and **ND** means you may not change it. |

We'll put a real CC-BY-SA license page next to a real CC0 page and you can tell me which one
obliges you to do something.

**The practical version of all of it:** when you find an asset, the question is never "does this
look free." It's "where is the license, and what does it say I have to do."

## IV. The AI Question

Two questions that are genuinely unsettled right now.

1. **Can AI companies train models on copyrighted work?** The New York Times is suing OpenAI and
   Microsoft over exactly this. It has not been decided.
2. **Can AI output be copyrighted?** Currently, mostly no. An AI cannot be an author. Which
   leaves an open question about who owns the output, if anyone does.

The one I want you arguing about:

> If a model learned from an artist's work, is the output a copy? Does it matter whether a human
> could have learned the same thing by studying that artist?

**The point:** you are entering this field while the rules are being written. Most people who
work in media inherit settled law. You won't, at least not for a while, and it's worth noticing
that you're arriving at an unusual moment.

The one practical rule, which is not unsettled: **disclose it.** In this course and in most
professional settings. That's a norm, not a school policy.
