## II. Useful Feedback or Just Venting?

I'm going to put some real negative reviews of a game you probably like on the screen.

For each one: is this **useful feedback**, or is it someone being mad on the internet? Hands up,
then tell me why you voted that way.

There's no trick. Some of them are genuinely useful and are still rude, and some are perfectly
polite and completely useless. That gap is the whole point.

The question that sorts them: **if you made this game, would this review change what you build
next?** If yes, it's critique. If no, it's noise, no matter how it's worded.

## III. What Makes a Critique Useful

You read Grant Wiggins for the study guide, so I'm not going to re-teach it. One frame, and then
we'll go use it.

**A useful critique makes a claim and supports it.** That's the same structure from the rhetoric
video, pointed at someone else's work:

| Not feedback | Feedback |
|---|---|
| "I didn't really like the market research" | "Your market research doesn't support your central claim. You say students want this, and the source you cite is about people over 40." |
| "The features section is confusing" | "I couldn't tell which three features are the core ones. Try leading with those and moving the rest to the appendix." |

The second column takes about fifteen more seconds to write, and it's the only kind anyone can act
on.

Three things worth keeping from Wiggins:

1. **Goal-referenced.** Judge it against what *they* were trying to build, not what you'd have
   built.
2. **Actionable.** They should know what to do Monday morning.
3. **Timely.** Which is why this is week 3 and not week 5. Feedback on something you can no longer
   change is just a review.

**One warning, because it's the thing that actually goes wrong Thursday.** The polite critique is
worthless. "Looks good, maybe add a bit more detail" helps nobody, and it's what everybody writes
the first time because being critical of strangers feels rude. Being vague isn't kind. It just
moves the problem to week 4, when it's expensive.

## IV. We Critique a Real Proposal

We're going to do this together first, on a real draft from last year's class. Names off.

It's a team that did fine in the end. We'll take one or two sections and run the same questions
you'll use on Thursday, and I'll push back on every vague answer, because that's what I'd do to
yours.

Two things to watch while we go:

- **How specific we have to get before the feedback is worth anything.** "This is weak" always
  needs a follow-up question. Skip straight to the follow-up.
- **Your own draft.** You're sitting there listening to a room pick apart someone else's executive
  summary. Yours is due tomorrow. That's not an accident.

## V. Draft Studio

The rest of class is yours to work on the draft.

I'll come around, and I'm asking every team the same question: **what's the thinnest section in
here right now?**

If you can answer it, you know what tonight is for. If you can't, we'll find it together, and
that's a better use of ten minutes than anything else I could do with them.

**If your team is behind**, don't start at the top. Write this sentence first:

> *[Product name]: a [what it is] that helps [who] [do what].*

Like: *LevelUpLife: a social rewards service that helps college students stop doomscrolling.*

A team that can write that sentence can write the rest of the proposal. A team that can't doesn't
have an idea yet, and Tuesday is a much better day to discover that than Friday.
