## II. What This Course Is (and Isn't)

Let's get this out of the way early: **this is not a game development course.**

If that's what you were hoping for, I understand. But here's what this actually is, and why
it's worth your attention.

This class is a big-picture overview of interactive media, which *includes* games but also
includes apps, websites, ads, installations, physical products, and the self-checkout machine
that yells at you about an unexpected item in the bagging area.

You'll learn how to:

- Research a topic properly, and tell a good source from a bad one
- Make an argument someone else finds convincing
- Communicate an idea visually, interactively, and on video
- Give and take criticism without it being miserable

**No textbook to buy.** Readings are on the web.

### How it's taught

The course is mostly **flipped**: you do readings and videos on your own, and class time goes
to activities, project work, and discussion. There are **four projects**, two in groups and
two on your own.

### Where to find things

There are three places, and each answers a different question:

| Where | What it answers |
|---|---|
| **The myCourses homepage** | What's due, and what should I read next? |
| **These weekly notes** | What did we actually do, and what were those links? |
| **Slides in class** | What are we talking about right now? |

These notes are the detailed reference. Come back to them after class. You don't need to read
along with them while class is happening.

### A note on how these notes work

These weekly notes are plain **Markdown files kept in a public GitHub repository**, pulled
into myCourses automatically. That's why you can read them right here instead of downloading
something.

I'm telling you this for two reasons.

First, compare it to the alternative. You've probably had a course where finding one detail
meant opening three PDFs and a Word document, scrolling until you spotted it. Here it's a
link in myCourses, it's searchable, and it's the same content every time.

Second, you're going to be living in Markdown and GitHub for the rest of this degree. IGME-235
and IGME-430 both assume it. Starting to notice how it works now costs you nothing.

If you'd rather read the raw files, or fork the repo and keep your own copy, you're welcome
to. We'll talk more about Markdown in a few weeks.

## IV. The Four Projects

| Project | Solo or Group | What you make |
|---|---|---|
| **P1: Product Proposal** | Group | A researched pitch for a product |
| **P2: Infographic** | Solo | A visual explainer for your product |
| **P3: Prototype** | Group (new teams) | A clickable prototype you test on real people |
| **P4: Video Ad** | Solo | A promotional video for your product |

They connect: P1 feeds P2 feeds P3 feeds P4.

Some real proposals from last year: *Bike Spikes* (traction control for bikes on winter
roads), *UniAid*, *Food Avenue*, *Excuse Genie*, *Taser Bed* (a bed that shocks you awake),
and *Anime Soap Lootboxes*.

Weird ideas are welcome. Lazy ideas are not.

## V. Syllabus: The Three Things That Matter

Full syllabus is in **myCourses → Content → Syllabus**. Read it. But these three come up the
most:

**In-class activities are 30% of your grade.** Most class days have one, and you do it and hand
it in during class. There is no separate attendance grade, so showing up is how you earn these.
Miss the class and there is nothing to submit.

**Late work:** request an extension in **#110-extension-request** on Slack, where there's a
workflow built for it, **at least 12 hours before** something is due. I'll usually say yes and you
don't need an elaborate excuse. Ask *after* the deadline and the answer is usually no.

**AI tools:** you're allowed to use them unless an assignment says otherwise. You **must**
disclose it: name the tool and say in a sentence what you used it for. No transcript needed.
Using AI is fine. Hiding it is an academic integrity violation.
