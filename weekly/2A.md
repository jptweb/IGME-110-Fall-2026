## II. Real or Fake?

We'll look at a couple of things and you vote: real, or generated?

Then, and this is the actual point, whoever voted has to say why. What convinced you it was real?
What tipped you off that it wasn't?

You'll do better on some of these than others. Where the room gets it wrong is the interesting
part, and it usually isn't the picture.

**The takeaway:** confidence is not evidence, and the check takes about thirty seconds. Hold onto
that. Thursday is the whole class built on it.

## III. What Project 1 Actually Asks

A researched proposal for a product that doesn't exist yet: a phone app, a web app, a website, a
desktop program, or a physical thing.

**→ [Full instructions: Project 1, Product Proposal](../documents/p1-product-proposal.md)**

We'll open that page together in class. It's long, and it's long on purpose: everything for the
whole project is on it, including both Google Doc templates, which sit *above* the deliverables.
Knowing the shape of the page now saves you asking where something is for the next four weeks.

Some real ones from last year: *Excuse Genie*, *Taser Bed* (a bed that shocks you awake),
*Bike Spikes* (traction control for bicycles on winter roads), *BetWise* (an app for people with
a gambling addiction).

Weird is welcome. **The idea is not the hard part.** The hard part is convincing a skeptical
reader that someone should actually fund this.

### The six sections, quickly

| | Section | The job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Executive Summary | What it's called, what it does, who it's for |
| 2 | Market Research and Need Analysis | Proof the problem is real |
| 3 | Product Description | What it is and how it works, plus mockups |
| 4 | Possible Issues and Counterarguments | The objections, answered honestly |
| 5 | Conclusion and Recommendations | Why someone should say yes |
| 6 | Appendix | Citations, extra data, AI disclosure |

### The one thing that decides your grade

Sections fail in exactly one way. They say true things that nobody has to believe.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Students are stressed about group scheduling" | "A 2024 survey of 1,200 undergrads found 68% missed at least one group meeting a month over scheduling conflicts" |
| "The apps out there aren't very good" | "When2meet sends no notifications, and three of the five people in this thread said they simply forgot to fill it in" |

**If you can't point to a source, it isn't evidence.** That is the difference between a section
that runs two lines and one that does its job, and it's most of what separates the proposals that
do well from the ones that don't.

Thursday is where you go get those sources.

## IV. Everyone Has a Job

These are jobs, not chapters. Each one runs the whole three weeks, and none of them means writing
a section alone.

| Role | Owns |
|---|---|
| Coordinator | The schedule and the check-ins. Who's doing what by when. |
| Editor | How it reads. One voice, and the final pass. |
| Evidence Lead | Every claim has something behind it. |
| Product Lead | What the thing actually is, and keeping the concept from drifting. |

*(Team of three? Coordinator and Editor fold together. Team of five? Add a second Evidence Lead.)*

**Two rules on top of that.** Everyone writes sections, handed out week by week in your work plan.
And everyone makes at least one mockup, because Section 3 needs three and there are four of you.

**Nobody is assigned to be the skeptic.** Instead the Coordinator runs five minutes at the end of
every meeting: what's the weakest thing in here right now? Those notes become Section 4, which is
the part that separates a proposal from a sales pitch.

## VI. Three Ideas Each

Study Guide 2 asked you to come out with three candidate topics. That's what you brought. Some of
you got there with AI, some didn't. Doesn't matter here.

The goal for the next eight minutes is to get everything out loud before anyone starts judging. A
team of four should end up with twelve ideas written down.

One way to run it, if you want a structure:

1. Each person reads their three ideas out loud
2. Hold the reactions until everyone has gone
3. One person writes them all down

Step 2 is the part worth protecting. If people start reacting on the second idea, ideas ten
through twelve never get said. Past that, run it however your team likes. Some of you already have
a brainstorming method that works for you, and I'd rather you use it than follow mine.

---

## VII. Cut It Down to One

Now you argue. Three questions that tend to settle it quickly:

- Is anyone actually excited about it?
- Could you find three real articles about it?
- Which one is the most fun to argue for?

**You are not stuck with the twelve.** If the conversation turns up something better, or a mashup
of two ideas nobody brought in on their own, take it. The list is there to get you started.

**You don't have to be certain today.** Friday's work plan is what locks the topic in, and
Thursday's research sometimes kills an idea, which is a good thing to find out on Thursday. If
your team is stuck, wave me over. Being stuck at this stage is normal and there's a trick for it.

## VIII. The Work Plan

Due **Friday, September 4**. Check myCourses for the exact time.

It asks for your product idea, who has which role, who's drafting what, the ideas you turned down,
and how your team plans to reach each other. It's short, and most teams can finish it before they
leave today. It exists because most group projects that go badly went badly in the first week,
when nobody said out loud who was doing what.

**One person submits it for the whole team**, and the submission is two links pasted into a text
box: your Work Plan doc and your proposal doc.

### Do this before you leave today

Your proposal doc has to exist by Thursday, because Thursday's activity ends with your team
loading research straight into it. Five minutes, three steps:

1. **One person copies both templates** and sets sharing on both
2. **Everyone else opens the proposal doc once**, right now, sitting here
3. If somebody can't get in, the sharing isn't set. Fix it while you're together

Step 2 is the one people skip, and it's the one that saves you Thursday. Opening it once puts it
in your Google Docs Recent list, so nobody is hunting for a link in the middle of an activity.

### Set sharing when you make the copies

Two settings on both docs, and then you're done thinking about it:

- **Your teammates: Editor**
- **General access: Anyone at RIT with the link, Viewer**

You don't need to share anything with me separately. I'm at RIT, so the link is enough. The second
setting is also what lets another team open your proposal in week 3 to critique it, so it isn't
optional.
