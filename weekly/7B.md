## I. This Is Hard, on Purpose

If your infographic is taking longer than you expected, you're not alone. Turning a wall of text
into something people get at a glance is hard, and there's no quick fix I can point to. In a coding
class I can look at your screen and say "you're missing a semicolon." Here there isn't one right
answer to find.

That's also why it's worth learning. Taking something complicated and making it clear for a
specific person comes up in almost every job, and it's hard to hand off. A tool can make something
look polished, but it doesn't know which five things your reader needs. And it's one of the few
places this semester where you get to make something look the way you want.

## II. What Counts as an Element

The brief asks for at least five elements. Here's how I count one:

- One piece of content a reader could take away on its own: a number, a comparison, a step, or a
  feature
- A label that says something specific
- An image that shows it

Not elements: the title, section headers, source lines, and decorative photos. An icon with one
word under it usually doesn't count either. "Fast" next to a lightning bolt doesn't tell the reader
anything. "Recipes in under 15 minutes" does.

It's a judgment call, and it's written into the brief now too:
[how I count an element](../documents/p2-infographic.md#what-the-infographic-must-contain).

In class we counted FriedPhish, the scam-warning infographic from Tuesday:

1. 73% of Americans have fallen victim to online scams, with a donut chart
2. 84% think people 65 and older are more at risk, with a bar chart
3. 68% are more worried about scams because of AI, with a gauge
4. A line chart of scam likelihood when using the app
5. Clear warnings when a scam is detected, with warning icons

Each stat and its chart count once, not twice. The title and the "Problem" and "Solution" headers
don't count. The paragraph under "The Solution" has no image of its own, so whether it counts is a
judgment call. Either way, it comes out to five.

## III. Peer Feedback

Today's activity is [Infographic Peer Feedback](../activities/infographic-peer-feedback.md).

- Groups of 3 or 4, so at least two other people see yours. If you end up in a pair, find another
  pair.
- Take turns. Say who it's for and the one thing it should leave them with, then let the others
  react.
- Count the elements, then go through what works, what's confusing, CRAP, and whether it would
  persuade the person it's for.

After class, write one short reflection (150 to 200 words, five questions on the activity page) and
hand it in on myCourses. That's the whole deliverable. You don't write up a separate review for
each person.

Whatever time is left is work time.
